## The Fallacy of Transition-level DP: Why Transition-Level Adjacency is Insufficient

The imperative to safeguard sensitive longitudinal records necessitates a rigorous paradigm for privacy-preserving offline reinforcement learning. Conventional methodologies overwhelmingly rely on transition-level clipping, a frequentist approach that treats each $(s, a, r, s')$ tuple as an independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample. Statistically, this assumption is profoundly flawed when applied to sequential decision-making. 

Let $g_{i,t} = \nabla_\theta \ell(s_{i,t}, a_{i,t}, r_{i,t}, s'_{i,t})$ denote the gradient of a single transition for trajectory $i$ at timestep $t$. In a Markov Decision Process (MDP), consecutive transitions are intrinsically temporally correlated, implying that the covariance matrix is strictly non-diagonal:

$$
\text{Cov}(g_{i,t}, g_{i,t+k}) \neq 0
$$

By artificially clipping individual transitions independently, the transition-level DP mechanism fundamentally underestimates the true sensitivity. The second moment of the trajectory gradient, $\mathbb{E}\left[ \left\| \sum_t g_{i,t} \right\|^2 \right]$, is heavily dominated by these positive cross-correlation terms. Consequently, individual transition clipping systematically understates the privacy leakage, failing to protect the true causal structure of the longitudinal log.

To rectify this theoretical inadequacy, we enforce trajectory-level differential privacy. By evaluating and bounding the sensitivity across the entirety of an episode ($\tau$), this framework natively aligns with the principle of *auditable minimisation*. Rather than fracturing a patient's medical history or a proprietary trading sequence into disjointed, statistically meaningless fragments, trajectory-level adjacency ensures that the mathematical influence of the entire longitudinal record is bounded. Formally, we define the trajectory-level sensitivity $\Delta$ as the maximum $L_2$-norm difference in gradients between any two adjacent trajectories, $\tau$ and $\tau'$:

$$
\Delta = \max_{\tau, \tau'} \left\| \nabla \ell(\tau) - \nabla \ell(\tau') \right\|_2
$$

This circumvents the escalating privacy budget accumulation invariably associated with cumulative transition-level exposures.

## Strategy 1: Influence Function Unlearning for Deep RL

Furthermore, to facilitate the computationally tractable removal of specific trajectories, we must address the highly non-convex nature of deep RL objectives. We integrate influence function-based unlearning. To approximate the influence of a deleted trajectory $\tau_k$ on the optimal parameters $\hat{\theta}$, we evaluate the inverse Hessian-vector product. To stabilise this within a non-convex landscape, we introduce a damped system:

$$
\left(H_{\hat{\theta}} + \lambda I\right)v = \nabla_\theta L_k(\hat{\theta})
$$

To efficiently solve this without explicitly materialising the prohibitive $O(d^2)$ Hessian $H_{\hat{\theta}}$, we employ the Linear Time Stochastic Second-Order Algorithm (LiSSA). By recursively estimating the inverse Hessian vector product, LiSSA establishes a mathematically rigorous and computationally viable mechanism for certified data deletion, eliminating the need for full policy retraining.

## Core Contribution: IQL vs CQL Deep Dive

The integration of differential privacy noise into offline reinforcement learning exposes a critical architectural vulnerability in prevailing conservative methodologies. Conservative Q-Learning (CQL) minimises a regularised objective featuring a penalty term $\mathcal{R}_{\text{CQL}}(\theta)$ that pushes down the Q-values of out-of-distribution (OOD) actions. 

When Rényi Differential Privacy (RDP) noise $\xi(s,a)$ is injected to protect the training data, the empirical critic becomes a noisy estimator: 

$$
\hat{Q}(s,a) = Q_\theta(s,a) + \xi(s,a)
$$

Because CQL actively seeks OOD actions to penalise the Q-function, it inherently queries the critic in regions where data is absent. In these regions, the superposition of DP noise onto the artificially constructed OOD targets aggressively exacerbates the Q-value estimation errors. By the properties of the sub-Gaussian union bound, the maximisation over these noisy, high-variance OOD samples leads to an exponential overestimation of the Bellman error. The noise variance scales catastrophically with the horizon during the Bellman backup, invariably culminating in a *catastrophic explosion* of the value function and subsequent policy collapse.

Conversely, Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) stands as the ultimate bastion of numerical stability under extreme privacy perturbations. IQL completely circumvents the OOD vulnerability by exclusively leveraging in-sample (in-distribution) data for policy evaluation. By formulating the value estimation strictly as an *expectile regression* problem, IQL avoids querying $\hat{Q}(s,a)$ for unseen actions. This structural property fundamentally alters the underlying optimisation landscape, suppressing the propagation of Gaussian noise during the Bellman contraction. Consequently, IQL demonstrates an extraordinary resilience to profound DP gradient perturbations, enabling the algorithm to maintain exceptional convergence properties even under stringent privacy constraints.

## Domain Generalisation: Bridging Healthcare and Finance

The robustness of the proposed framework was empirically validated across two fundamentally disparate operational domains: the MIMIC-III healthcare proxy and the FinRL financial proxy. 

The healthcare domain is notoriously characterised by highly stochastic, sparse rewards, where patient physiological trajectories exhibit profound variance. Conversely, the financial domain operates within a high Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) environment, demanding meticulous exploitation of minute alpha signals. Our trajectory-level DP framework, fortified by the numerical stability of IQL, successfully bridged this generalisation gap. It seamlessly protected highly sensitive ICU biometric indicators and proprietary trading strategies alike, without requiring domain-specific algorithmic recalibration.
